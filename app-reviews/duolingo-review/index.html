Duolingo Review 2026: An Honest Look After Years of Daily Use

By Elena | March 20, 2026

Quick Verdict: "Duolingo is the best free starting point for language learning — but it's getting harder to love."

The app excels at building daily habits, teaching vocabulary, and making language learning feel approachable rather than intimidating. It's genuinely fun, especially in the early stages. But the 2025 energy system has made the free experience significantly worse, the gamification can become more addictive than educational, and Duolingo alone won't get you to fluency. It's a fantastic appetizer — just don't mistake it for the full meal.

What Is Duolingo?

If you somehow haven't heard of it — Duolingo is a free language learning app with a green owl mascot who will guilt-trip you into practicing daily. It launched in 2011 and has since grown to over 500 million registered users learning 40+ languages through bite-sized, gamified lessons.

But the Duolingo of 2026 is a very different product from the one many people signed up for a few years ago. Here are the biggest changes:

  • The energy system (rolled out in 2025): Replaced the old hearts system. Every exercise drains energy — even if you answer perfectly. Free users can run out after just a few lessons and face waits of up to 18 hours to recharge. This is, by far, the most controversial change in Duolingo's history.
  • Duolingo Max: A premium tier ($29.99/month) that unlocks AI-powered Video Calls with characters like Lily and Falstaff, plus Roleplay conversations. Currently supports Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Japanese, Korean, and Chinese.
  • Explain My Answer: An AI feature that gives you personalized grammar explanations when you get something wrong. It was previously Max-only, but as of January 2026, it's free for all users — one of the smartest moves Duolingo has made recently.
  • Content expansion: In April 2025, Duolingo launched 148 new language courses, making major languages like Japanese and Korean available from more native language interfaces.

My Experience Using Duolingo

I'll be honest: my relationship with Duolingo is complicated. It's the app I've used most consistently for language learning over the past couple of years, and that counts for something. Very few apps can make you want to open them every single day.

In my experience, Duolingo is at its best in the first few months. The lessons feel fresh, the progress feels real, and the gamification hits that sweet spot of motivation. I genuinely learned vocabulary and basic sentence structures that stuck. The spacing algorithm does its job — words I struggled with kept coming back until they finally clicked.

Where it started to frustrate me was around the intermediate stage. I'd been doing lessons daily for months, my streak was climbing, but I noticed I was getting better at Duolingo rather than better at the language. I could ace the exercises, but I still froze when trying to form an original sentence. That gap between app performance and real-world ability is Duolingo's fundamental challenge.

"It's the only app that actually got me to practice every single day. I've never been consistent with anything else."

— Reddit user, r/duolingo

I relate to that completely. Duolingo nailed the habit-building piece. The question is whether the habit it builds is "learning a language" or "completing Duolingo lessons."

Key Features Breakdown

Gamification: Streaks, XP, and Leaderboards

Duolingo's gamification is both its superpower and its Achilles' heel.

The streak is the star. It's absurdly effective at getting you to show up every day. Duolingo's own data shows that learners who hit a 7-day streak are 2.4 times more likely to continue the next day. Once you're at 30, 100, or 365 days, losing that streak feels genuinely painful — which is exactly the point. Loss aversion is a powerful motivator, and Duolingo exploits it masterfully.

But here's the dark side: the streak can become the goal instead of the learning. Streak freezes let you maintain your number without opening the app. XP leaderboards incentivize speed-running easy lessons rather than tackling harder material.

"I found myself doing lessons at 11:59 PM out of anxiety about losing a 500-day streak, despite barely being able to order food in the target language."

— Duolingo user

That quote captures a real tension. The gamification keeps you engaged, but engagement and learning aren't always the same thing. My advice: use the streak as a tool, not a trophy. If you catch yourself doing the absolute minimum just to keep the number alive, that's a sign to reassess.

That said, not all gamification is bad. I know people who replaced hours of social media scrolling with Duolingo practice — and even if the learning is imperfect, that's a trade-up.

"I deleted most of my social media and replaced it with Duolingo. I used to spend about 7 hours a day on social media. At least now I'm learning something."

— Reddit user, r/duolingo

Course Content and Teaching Method

Duolingo's teaching approach is primarily translation-based. You see a sentence in your target language, you translate it (or vice versa), and the app tells you if you're right. It's supplemented with listening exercises, word-matching, and fill-in-the-blank questions.

For vocabulary acquisition, this works well. The spaced repetition system ensures that words you struggle with keep appearing until they stick. For beginners, it's an effective way to build a foundation — you'll pick up common words, basic sentence patterns, and get a feel for how the language sounds.

Where it falls short is grammar depth. Duolingo doesn't explicitly teach grammar rules — you're expected to absorb patterns from examples. For some learners, this intuitive approach works. For others, it's deeply frustrating to keep getting something wrong without understanding why. The newly free "Explain My Answer" feature helps here, giving AI-powered grammar explanations when you make a mistake. It's available for Spanish, French, German, Japanese, Portuguese, Italian, and Korean, and it's a meaningful improvement.

"Duolingo is great at getting you started, but after a while you realize you're just translating sentences and not actually thinking in the language."

— Reddit user, r/languagelearning

That observation resonates. Duolingo can teach you to decode a language, but thinking and speaking in it requires different practice.

Speaking and Listening Practice

Duolingo includes speaking exercises where you read a sentence aloud and the app evaluates your pronunciation. These are... okay. The speech recognition is functional but far from perfect — it sometimes accepts clearly wrong pronunciation and occasionally rejects correct attempts.

The real upgrade in this area is Duolingo Max's Video Call feature. You can have AI-powered conversations with characters like Lily (free-form conversation) and Falstaff (guided, coached conversations). Currently available for Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Japanese, Korean, and Chinese — it's the closest Duolingo gets to actual conversational practice.

Is it as good as talking to a real person? No. But it's available at 2 AM in your pajamas with zero social anxiety, and that accessibility matters. For learners who are too shy or don't have access to native speakers, it's a genuinely useful stepping stone.

Energy System and Ads: The Elephant in the Room

Let's talk about the energy system, because this is where Duolingo has genuinely lost user trust.

In 2025, Duolingo replaced its hearts system with an energy system where every exercise drains energy — even if you answer perfectly. Free users get 25 energy units, and once they're gone, you either watch ads, spend gems, do practice lessons, or wait up to 18 hours for a full recharge.

The old hearts system was annoying but logical: make mistakes, lose hearts. The energy system punishes you for using the app at all. That's a fundamentally different contract with the user.

"I can do three perfect lessons with zero mistakes, and I'm suddenly forced to wait 18 hours."

— Duolingo user

The community backlash has been severe. A Reddit post titled "So Now We're Punished for Using the App?" got nearly 3,000 upvotes. An analysis of 1,000 Play Store reviews found that 45% of negative sentiment stems directly from the energy system. Surveys show 61% of users dislike it. Long-term users — the people who were most loyal to the platform — now rate the app an average of 2.32 stars.

"I had over 700 days of streaks and I quit because I couldn't even complete my daily missions anymore."

— Long-term user, cited in Android Authority

I understand why Duolingo did this — they need revenue, and only about 6% of users pay for subscriptions while generating 72% of revenue. But the execution feels like it prioritizes squeezing free users rather than finding a sustainable model that doesn't actively hinder learning.

Super Duolingo and Max subscribers get unlimited energy, so if you're a paying customer, this doesn't affect you. But for the vast majority of users who rely on the free tier, it's a significant downgrade in the learning experience.

Duolingo Max: AI Features

Duolingo Max ($29.99/month or $167.99/year) unlocks the platform's most advanced features:

  • Video Call with Lily: Unguided conversation practice — you pick a scenario and chat freely. Available for English, Spanish, French, German, Italian, Portuguese, Japanese, and Korean.
  • Video Call with Falstaff: Guided conversations with coaching and feedback. Available for the above plus Chinese.
  • Roleplay: Scenario-based conversations in everyday situations.

These features represent Duolingo's most serious attempt at conversational practice, and they're genuinely impressive from a technology standpoint. But at $29.99/month, Max is expensive — especially when competitors like apps offering unlimited AI conversation practice exist at lower price points.

If Duolingo Max mainly interests you because of AI speaking practice, compare it with Langua before paying. Langua is built more directly around open-ended AI conversation, Call Mode, and post-chat feedback, while Duolingo Max sits inside a broader gamified course.

The good news: Explain My Answer, previously a Max-exclusive feature, became free for all users in January 2026. This is one of Duolingo's most useful AI features, and making it free was a smart move that partially offsets the frustration around the energy system.

Pros and Cons

Pros

  • Unmatched habit-building: The streak system and daily notifications genuinely get people to practice consistently — something most language learning methods fail at
  • Beautiful, intuitive design: The UI is polished, playful, and immediately understandable, even for non-tech-savvy users
  • 40+ languages for free: From Spanish and French to Hawaiian and High Valyrian — the breadth of language options is unmatched
  • Effective for beginners: The first few months of Duolingo provide a genuinely solid foundation in vocabulary and basic structures
  • Explain My Answer is now free: AI-powered grammar explanations for mistakes — available to all users since January 2026
  • Social media replacement: For many users, Duolingo is a productive substitute for mindless scrolling

Cons

  • Energy system actively hinders learning: Free users are punished for practicing — even perfect lessons drain energy, with 18-hour waits to recharge
  • Gamification can be counterproductive: Streaks and XP incentivize minimum effort and easy lessons over meaningful challenge
  • Won't build conversational fluency alone: Translation exercises don't teach you to think or speak in the language
  • Grammar instruction is shallow: You're expected to absorb rules by pattern rather than understanding them — frustrating for many adult learners
  • Increasingly aggressive monetization: The free experience has degraded significantly, feeling more like a trial version than a genuine free product
  • Speaking exercises are mediocre: Basic speech recognition that's neither reliable enough to trust nor challenging enough to help

Who Is Duolingo Best For?

Complete beginners wanting to test the waters with a new language: Perfect. Duolingo's gentle learning curve and zero-commitment entry point make it the ideal first step. Download it, try a few lessons, see if the language clicks.

Casual learners wanting to build a daily habit in spare moments: Great fit. If your goal is "learn some Italian before my trip" or "pick up basic Japanese for fun," Duolingo delivers. Pair it with music, shows, or podcasts in your target language and you'll make real progress.

Serious learners wanting to reach conversational fluency: Not enough on its own. Use Duolingo as one tool among many — a daily warm-up, not your entire workout. You'll need conversation practice, grammar study, and immersion content to get there.

"I completed my second foreign language in two years using Duolingo, but I also took classes at a language school. Duolingo alone wouldn't have gotten me there."

— Reddit user, r/duolingo

Budget-conscious learners who can't pay for anything: Usable, but frustrating. The free version still works, but the energy system means your daily practice time is capped. Web-based practice and practice lessons can help you stretch your energy further.

Kids learning a language: Solid choice. The gamification that can feel manipulative to adults is genuinely fun and motivating for younger learners. Duolingo also offers a dedicated kids experience.

Who should skip it: If you're already at an intermediate level and want to push toward advanced proficiency, Duolingo probably isn't where you should be spending your time. Also, if gamification and notifications stress you out rather than motivate you, this app will feel more like a chore than a tool.

How Duolingo Compares

Duolingo vs Babbel: Babbel offers more structured, grammar-focused lessons and feels more like a traditional course. It's better for learners who want explicit explanations, but it lacks Duolingo's free tier and habit-building gamification. If you want depth over breadth, Babbel might be your pick. Read my full Babbel review or the Duolingo vs Babbel comparison.

Duolingo vs Busuu: Busuu is less gamified but stronger for structured grammar, writing prompts, and community feedback from real people. If Duolingo keeps you consistent but leaves you guessing why sentences work, Busuu is one of the more natural next apps to test.

Duolingo vs Rosetta Stone: Rosetta Stone uses full immersion (no translations), which some learners love and others find frustrating. It's pricier and has fewer languages, but the immersive method can build stronger intuitive skills. Read the full Duolingo vs Rosetta Stone comparison, or use the Duolingo vs Babbel vs Rosetta Stone comparison for a detailed 3-way breakdown.

Duolingo vs Pimsleur: Pimsleur is audio-based and excels at speaking and listening — exactly where Duolingo is weakest. The two actually complement each other well if your budget allows.

In general, Duolingo's biggest competitive advantage is that it's free and fun. No other app matches its ability to get complete beginners started with zero friction. Where it loses ground is depth, grammar, and conversational practice.
